# Global Convergence, National Divergence: A Verified Data Briefing, 1820 to 2035

## Section 1: Document Scope and Measurement Conventions

### 1.1 Core thesis in three statements

- Between-country inequality has collapsed since 1980, driven mainly by China and India.
- Within-country inequality has risen over the same period, most visibly in the United States.
- Both trends are real, they measure different distributions, and neither cancels the other.

### 1.2 Which "top 1 percent" is meant

This document uses three distinct percentile groups. They are not interchangeable.

- **Global top 1 percent by income:** requires roughly $60,000 to $70,000 in annual income per person, purchasing-power adjusted. A typical rich-country middle-class household does **not** reach this group.
- **Global top 3 to 8 percent by income:** where a self-described "squeezed middle class" household in a high-income country typically sits.
- **US top 1 percent by wealth:** a national group holding about 30.8 percent of US household net worth in 2024.

### 1.3 Poverty lines and PPP vintages

- **Current headline series used here:** $2.15 per day, 2017 purchasing power parity.
- **Retired series:** $1.90 per day, 2011 purchasing power parity. Figures from this series are about 1 to 2 percentage points lower than the $2.15 series for the same year and must not be mixed into a single trend line.
- **June 2025 re-basing:** the World Bank moved the international poverty line to $3.00 per day, 2021 purchasing power parity. This raises measured headcounts without any change in living conditions.

### 1.4 Other conventions

- "Gini" refers to the global interpersonal Gini coefficient unless a country is named.
- Gross domestic product comparisons are nominal unless labelled purchasing power parity.
- Wealth and income are separate distributions and are labelled separately throughout.
- Ranges such as "75 to 80 percent" indicate genuine estimate uncertainty, not a change over time.

## Section 2: The Perception Gap

### 2.1 What surveyed publics believe

- **Gapminder 2017, three-option question across 14 countries:** 7 percent of respondents correctly answered that the share of humanity in extreme poverty had almost halved over the previous two decades.
- **United States correct-answer rate:** about 5 percent.
- **Random-guess benchmark:** 33 percent, because the question had three options. Respondents performed roughly five times worse than chance.
- **Majority belief in most high-income countries:** that global poverty rose during the largest poverty reduction ever recorded.
- **Norton and Ariely wealth-perception study, 2011:** Americans estimated the top quintile held 59 percent of national wealth, said the ideal share would be 32 percent, and were surveyed against an actual figure of about 84 percent. The estimate understated reality by about 25 percentage points.

### 2.2 What the data show: convergence between countries

- **Extreme poverty rate, $2.15 per day:** 37.9 percent in 1990; about 9.0 percent in 2022; about 8.5 percent in the 2024 nowcast.
- **Extreme poverty headcount:** about 2.02 billion in 1990; about 700 million in 2024. A decline of about 1.3 billion.
- **World population over the same window:** 5.32 billion in 1990 to about 8.2 billion in 2024, an increase of about 2.9 billion. The headcount fell while the denominator grew by more than half.
- **Average exit rate:** about 39 million people per year, about 106,000 per day, about 1.2 per second, sustained for 34 years.
- **Global life expectancy at birth:** 62.8 years in 1980 to 73.2 years in 2023, a gain of 10.4 years in 43 years.
- **Deaths of children under five:** about 12.5 to 12.8 million in 1990 to about 4.8 million in 2023, a decline of about 62 percent.
- **Global interpersonal Gini:** about 0.69 to 0.70 across the 1988 to 2008 plateau, falling to about 0.62 by 2018. This is the first sustained decline since industrialization began separating world regions around 1820.

### 2.3 What the data show: divergence within countries

- **US top 1 percent wealth share:** about 23 percent in 1989 to about 30.8 percent in 2024, a rise of about 7 to 8 percentage points.
- **US bottom 50 percent wealth share:** about 3.5 percent in 1989 to about 2.5 percent in 2024.
- **Full 2024 US distribution:** top 1 percent about 30.8 percent; next 9 percent about 36 percent; 50th to 90th percentiles about 30.5 percent; bottom 50 percent about 2.5 percent.
- **Real median earnings, US men working full time year round:** approximately flat from 1973 to 2023, with cumulative change in the low single digits across five decades.

### 2.4 Why both pictures are true

- The collapse in inequality happened **between** countries.
- The increase in inequality happened **within** countries.
- A household can therefore rise sharply in the world ranking while standing still in its own national ranking, and people experience the national ranking daily.

## Section 3: Chronology of the Pivot, 1800 to 2025

### 3.1 The geography decomposition

Share of global inequality attributable to which country a person lives in, from the World Inequality Report 2022 Theil decomposition:

- **1820:** 11 percent. Inequality was overwhelmingly local, a matter of class within villages.
- **1980:** 57 percent, the all-time peak. The "developed versus developing" frame was statistically accurate.
- **2020:** 32 percent. A fall of 25 percentage points, a relative decline of about 44 percent in 40 years.
- **Crossing below 50 percent:** around the year 2000.

### 3.2 Milestone timeline

- **1800:** About 80 to 90 percent of humanity lived below the modern extreme-poverty threshold. The distribution was unimodal, with no rich-world second mode.
- **1975:** Fully bimodal. Hans Rosling's "two-humped camel," with a poor-world mode and a rich-world mode separated by a real valley.
- **1978 to 1980, convergence trigger:** Deng Xiaoping's reforms, the household responsibility system, and the first Special Economic Zones. China's nominal gross domestic product per person was about $195, roughly 1.6 percent of the US level of about $12,550.
- **1981 to 1988, rich-world counter-trigger:** the US top marginal income tax rate fell from 70 percent to 50 percent in 1982 and to 28 percent by 1988. US private-sector union density fell from 24.2 percent in 1973 to 16.8 percent in 1983 and to 5.9 percent in 2024.
- **1990, baseline year:** about 2.02 billion people in extreme poverty. East Asia and Pacific poverty rate about 65 percent. China's rate about 72 percent on the $2.15 series and about 66 percent on the retired $1.90 series.
- **1991, second convergence trigger:** India's liberalization, including rupee devaluation and dismantling of the licence raj. India's extreme-poverty rate was about 48 to 50 percent.
- **1999 to 2011, rich-world counter-shock:** Autor, Dorn, and Hanson attribute 2.0 to 2.4 million lost US jobs to import competition from China, concentrated in the same workers who form the flat section of the global growth curve.
- **December 2001:** China joins the World Trade Organization. Containerization and fibre-optic bandwidth collapse the cost of coordinating production across a wage gap of roughly 20 to 1.
- **2008:** Global Gini still about 0.70. The Lakner and Milanovic elephant curve ends here, and its trunk is already flat.
- **2013 to 2018:** Global Gini falls to about 0.62. China's gross domestic product per person, purchasing power adjusted, reaches about 26 to 28 percent of the US level by 2018 and about 29 to 33 percent by 2024. China's share of world output, purchasing power adjusted, rises from about 2 percent in 1980 to about 19 percent in 2024.
- **September 2018, structural crossover:** Brookings researchers Kharas and Hamel date the point at which a majority of humanity, about 3.8 billion of 7.6 billion people, is middle class or wealthier.
- **2020, reversal:** COVID-19 pushes global extreme poverty up by 70 to 90 million people, the first increase since 1998, and between-country inequality rises for the first time in a generation. Estimates also show the global middle class shrinking that year.
- **2024:** About 700 million people in extreme poverty, about 8.5 percent of humanity.
- **June 2025, measurement change:** the World Bank re-bases the international poverty line to $3.00 per day, 2021 purchasing power parity. The 2022 headcount is restated from about 713 million, or 9.0 percent, to 838 million, or 10.5 percent. The added 125 million people reflect a change in the yardstick, not a change in welfare.

## Section 4: Winners, Losers, and Outliers

### 4.1 The global ledger

- **World income shares:** top 10 percent take 52 percent; middle 40 percent take 39.5 percent; bottom 50 percent take 8.5 percent.
- **Per-person income comparison:** the average person in the top decile receives about 31 times the income of the average person in the bottom half.
- **World wealth shares:** top 10 percent hold about 76 percent; middle 40 percent hold about 22 percent; bottom 50 percent hold about 2 percent.
- **Per-person wealth comparison:** the average person in the top decile holds roughly 190 times the wealth of the average person in the bottom half.
- **Capture of global wealth growth, 1995 to 2021:** the top 1 percent captured 38 percent; the bottom 50 percent captured 2 percent.

### 4.2 The elephant's trunk

Cumulative real income growth by global percentile, 1988 to 2008:

- **Global median:** 75 to 80 percent total, about 2.9 percent per year.
- **Global top 1 percent:** about 65 percent total.
- **80th to 85th global percentile, largely the Western working and lower-middle class:** 0 to 2 percent total, about 0.05 percent per year.
- **Ratio of annual growth rates:** about 58 to 1 in favour of the world's median over two decades.
- **Composition caveat:** Adam Corlett of the Resolution Foundation showed that Japan's stagnant decade and the post-Soviet collapse occupy much of the trunk. Holding country composition fixed lifts the trunk but does not eliminate it.

### 4.3 Scale comparisons

- **US top 1 percent net worth, 2024:** about $49 trillion, being 30.8 percent of about $160 trillion in household net worth on the Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts basis.
- **US bottom 50 percent net worth, 2024:** about $4 trillion. The ratio is about 12 to 1.
- **International comparison:** $49 trillion is roughly 1.6 times the combined 2024 nominal gross domestic product of China, Japan, Germany, and India, which totals about $31 trillion.
- **The domestic shift since 1989:** the 7.3 percentage point rise in the top 1 percent share equals about $12 trillion relative to the 1989 distribution, or roughly $91,000 for each of about 132 million US households.
- **China's poverty exit:** about 800 million people, roughly equal to the combined 2024 populations of the United States at 342 million, Brazil at 213 million, Japan at 124 million, Germany at 84 million, and the United Kingdom at 69 million.

### 4.4 The price split: why a winner can feel like a loser

US consumer price changes since 2000, with the all-items index as the benchmark:

- **All items, benchmark:** up about 82 percent.
- **Televisions:** down about 95 percent or more.
- **Toys:** down about 70 percent.
- **Clothing:** flat to slightly negative.
- **Hospital services:** up about 230 percent.
- **College tuition and fees:** up about 180 percent.
- **Childcare and nursery school:** up about 115 percent.

Related security costs:

- **US median home price to median income:** about 3.2 times in the 1990s to about 5.0 to 5.8 times in 2022 to 2024.
- **US health spending per person:** $2,843 in 1990 to about $14,570 in 2023, about 5.1 times in nominal terms, and from 12.1 percent to 17.6 percent of gross domestic product.
- **US CEO to typical worker pay, realized compensation basis:** about 20 to 1 in 1965; about 31 to 1 in 1978; about 59 to 1 in 1989; about 399 to 1 in 2021; about 344 to 1 in 2022. The 2021 spike reflects stock-option exercises, so single-year readings are volatile.

Globalization cheapened the tradable goods that signal comfort and did nothing to slow the non-tradable services that constitute security.

### 4.5 The outlier inside the growth engine

- **Real median earnings, US men without a bachelor's degree, 1979 to 2019:** an estimated decline of 10 to 17 percent.
- **US real gross domestic product over the same window:** up about 2.8 to 2.9 times, roughly tripled.
- **Deaths of despair:** about 158,000 per year as of 2017, which Case and Deaton compared to about 433 deaths per day, the equivalent of three fully loaded Boeing 737 aircraft crashing daily.
- **US life expectancy:** declined in 2015, 2016, and 2017, a pattern not shared by other high-income countries in that period.

### 4.6 The geographic outlier

- **Sub-Saharan Africa poverty rate:** about 55 percent in 1990 to about 35 percent in 2024.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa poverty headcount:** 284 million in 1990 to about 465 million in 2024, an increase of about 64 percent.
- **Regional population, the reconciling variable:** about 510 million in 1990 to about 1.3 billion in 2024. A falling rate and a rising headcount are consistent because the denominator more than doubled.
- **Share of the world's extreme poor:** about 14 percent in 1990 to about 66 percent in 2024. Poverty relocated rather than vanished.

## Section 5: The 2030 Horizon and Second-Order Effects

### 5.1 Poverty and middle-class projections

- **Extreme poverty in 2030, World Bank baseline:** about 7.3 percent, or about 620 million people, against a Sustainable Development Goal target of 3 percent. The projected miss is a factor of about 2.4.
- **Higher-line horizon:** eliminating poverty at the $6.85 per day line, 2017 purchasing power parity, would take more than a century at current growth rates.
- **Geographic concentration by 2030:** roughly 9 in 10 of the world's extreme poor are projected to live in Sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Global middle class in 2030:** about 5.3 billion people, roughly 62 percent of projected world population, compared with just over 50 percent in 2018.
- **Regional source of growth:** about 88 percent of the next billion entrants to the global middle class are projected to be Asian.

### 5.2 The convergence engine slows

- **China's population peak:** 1.4126 billion in 2021, declining every year since, with a shrinking working-age cohort.
- **IMF baseline growth:** about 4.0 percent for emerging markets and developing economies against about 1.7 percent for advanced economies.
- **Convergence spread:** about 2.3 percentage points, roughly half the spread of about 4.3 points seen in the 2000s.
- **Excluding China:** the per-person growth premium for emerging markets narrows to about 1 to 1.5 percentage points.

### 5.3 The technology inversion

- **IMF 2024 estimates of job exposure to artificial intelligence:** 60 percent in advanced economies, 40 percent in emerging markets, 26 percent in low-income countries. Exposure is highest where capital already sits.
- **Premature deindustrialization, per Dani Rodrik:** manufacturing employment peaks near 13 percent of total employment in India and near 16 percent in Brazil, against about 35 to 45 percent in the historical United Kingdom and about 40 percent in historical Germany. The industrial employment ladder is being sawn off at a lower rung.
- **Labour income share:** the International Labour Organization records a global decline of about 0.6 percentage points from 2019 to 2022.

### 5.4 Second-order effects

- **Politics:** re-nationalizes around distribution rather than growth, because the domestic ranking is the one voters feel.
- **Trade policy:** tariffs and industrial policy displace liberalization as the default instrument.
- **Migration pressure:** intensifies as the place premium for observably identical workers runs from about 4 times at the median to about 15 times at the extreme.
- **Fiscal capacity:** about 3.3 billion people live in countries that spend more on debt interest than on education or health.

### 5.5 The structural threshold

- **Between-country share of global inequality:** fell below 50 percent around 2000 and reached 32 percent by 2020.
- **Linear extrapolation, 1980 to 2020 trend of about minus 0.63 points per year:** reaches 25 percent around 2031.
- **Linear extrapolation, 2000 to 2020 trend of about minus 0.9 points per year:** reaches 25 percent around 2028.
- **Interpretation:** once the between-country share falls below about 25 percent, effectively all incremental global inequality is generated inside national borders, and no amount of foreign convergence can improve how the median rich-country citizen ranks at home.
- **Reversibility caveat:** this threshold is structural, not irreversible. The 2020 pandemic reversed both the poverty trend and the between-country trend for at least two years, which shows that these series can move backward.

## Appendix A: Internal Arithmetic Checks

Every relationship below was recomputed and holds within rounding.

- **Poverty headcount, 1990:** 37.9 percent of 5.32 billion equals about 2.02 billion.
- **Poverty headcount, 2024:** 8.5 percent of about 8.2 billion equals about 700 million.
- **Total decline:** 2.02 billion minus 0.70 billion equals about 1.32 billion.
- **Exit rate:** 1.32 billion divided by 34 years equals about 39 million per year, about 106,000 per day, about 1.2 per second.
- **Geography's lost explanatory power:** 57 percent minus 32 percent equals 25 percentage points, which is 43.9 percent of 57 percent.
- **Elephant curve annualization:** 77.5 percent cumulative over 20 years equals 2.87 percent per year; 1 percent cumulative over 20 years equals 0.05 percent per year; the ratio is about 58 to 1.
- **US wealth ratio:** 30.8 percent divided by 2.5 percent equals about 12.3 to 1, and $49 trillion divided by $4 trillion equals about 12.3 to 1. The two methods agree.
- **The domestic shift:** 7.3 percent of about $160 trillion equals about $11.7 trillion, and $11.7 trillion divided by 132 million households equals about $89,000 per household.
- **China's poverty exit versus five nations:** 342 plus 213 plus 124 plus 84 plus 69 equals about 832 million, against about 800 million people lifted out of extreme poverty.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa rate reconciliation:** 284 million of 510 million equals 55.7 percent for 1990; 465 million of 1.31 billion equals 35.5 percent for 2024. The falling rate and rising headcount are internally consistent.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa share of world poor:** 284 million of 2.02 billion equals 14.1 percent; 465 million of 700 million equals 66.4 percent.
- **2030 projection:** 7.3 percent of a projected 8.5 billion equals about 620 million, and 7.3 divided by 3 equals a shortfall factor of 2.4.
- **Deaths of despair comparison:** 158,000 divided by 365 equals 433 per day, which matches three aircraft of about 144 seats each.
- **Convergence spread:** 4.0 percent minus 1.7 percent equals 2.3 percentage points, which is about 53 percent of the 4.3-point spread of the 2000s.

## Appendix C: Measurement Traps and Common Misreadings

- **Do not splice poverty series.** A 2015 rate of 10.1 percent belongs to the retired $1.90 line, 2011 purchasing power parity. On the current $2.15 line the 2015 rate is about 11 percent. Mixing them creates a fictitious drop.
- **Do not read the 2025 re-basing as deterioration.** Moving to $3.00 per day, 2021 purchasing power parity, raised the 2022 count by about 125 million people through arithmetic alone.
- **Do not attach 2022 and 2024 poverty figures to the same line.** The 838 million figure is the 2022 count at $3.00 per day. The 700 million figure is the 2024 nowcast at $2.15 per day.
- **Do not date the 20 to 1 CEO pay ratio to 1978.** That ratio belongs to 1965. The 1978 figure is about 31 to 1.
- **Do not confuse union density measures.** The 16.8 percent figure for 1983 is private-sector density. The all-sector rate that year was 20.1 percent.
- **Do not date the global wealth-growth capture to 1980 to 2020.** The 38 percent and 2 percent capture shares cover 1995 to 2021.
- **Do not describe a rich-country middle-class household as the global 1 percent.** Such households sit in the global top 3 to 8 percent by income.
- **Do not mix nominal and purchasing-power comparisons of China with the United States.** China reached about 26 to 28 percent of US output per person by 2018 on a purchasing-power basis, but well under 20 percent on a nominal basis.
- **Do not treat the elephant's trunk as purely a Western phenomenon.** Japan's stagnation and the post-Soviet collapse account for a meaningful part of it.
- **Do not call any threshold irreversible.** Both the poverty trend and the between-country trend reversed in 2020.
- **Do not read the 44 percent decline in the between-country share as 44 percentage points.** It is a 25-point fall from a 57 percent base.
- **Do not treat the 84 percent top-quintile wealth figure as current.** It is the benchmark used in the 2011 study, drawn from 2004 survey data; later estimates are higher.

## Appendix D: Figures Requiring Re-verification Before Publication

- **The 2015 extreme-poverty rate on the $2.15 series.** Published values across Poverty and Inequality Platform vintages range from about 10.5 to 12 percent. Pull the current value directly.
- **The labour-income-share monetary equivalent.** A 0.6 percentage point shift equals about $0.7 trillion per year against 2024 global nominal output of about $110 trillion, or about $1.2 trillion per year on a purchasing-power basis. Any figure near $2.4 trillion is therefore cumulative rather than annual, and the International Labour Organization wording should be quoted exactly.
- **Any 2025 poverty nowcast, including figures near 808 million.** Confirm the reference year and the line used.
- **The 10 to 17 percent decline in real earnings for US men without a bachelor's degree.** The result is sensitive to the deflator, the age window, and whether non-earners are included.
- **Peak manufacturing employment shares for the United Kingdom and Germany.** Values near 45 percent generally reflect all industry rather than manufacturing alone.
- **US household net worth totals.** The Distributional Financial Accounts and the Financial Accounts of the United States differ by several percent, which scales all dollar levels in Section 4.3 by a similar margin.
- **The upper poverty lines after re-basing.** The $6.85 line, 2017 purchasing power parity, corresponds to a re-based line near $8.30, 2021 purchasing power parity. Confirm before quoting century-scale timelines.
- **The 1989 bottom 50 percent US wealth share of about 3.5 percent** and the intermediate 2024 shares for the 90th to 99th and 50th to 90th percentile groups.
